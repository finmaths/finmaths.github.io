Lesson 3: From Gross Pay to Take-Home Pay

Year level: Year 10 (middle ability set)Duration: 60 minutesFocal concept/topic: Earning money: pay periods, leave loading, PAYG tax and net pay

Lesson aim

Convert pay between weekly, fortnightly, monthly and yearly amounts, calculate leave loading, and use the ATO weekly tax table to find take-home pay, so that students can answer Lesson 1's question "Which job pays the most?"

Learning intention

We are learning to convert pay between time periods, calculate leave loading, and use the ATO tax table to work out net pay.

Success criteria

□ I can convert pay between weekly, fortnightly, monthly and yearly amounts using 1 year = 52 weeks, and explain why monthly pay is not 4 × weekly pay
□ I can calculate leave loading as a percentage of normal pay for the leave period
□ I can read the ATO weekly tax table to find the tax withheld
□ I can calculate net pay by subtracting the tax withheld from gross pay

Syllabus outcomes and content

NSW Education Standards Authority (NESA, 2022)

  • MA5-FIN-C-01: solves financial problems involving simple interest, earning money and spending money
  • MAO-WM-01: develops understanding and fluency in mathematics through exploring and connecting mathematical concepts, choosing and applying mathematical techniques to solve problems, and communicating their thinking and reasoning coherently and clearly
  • Content:
    • Calculate weekly, fortnightly, monthly and yearly earnings assuming 1 year = 52 weeks
    • Calculate leave loading by finding a percentage of eligible normal pay
    • Investigate sources of published tables or online calculators and use these to calculate the weekly, fortnightly or monthly tax to be deducted from a worker's pay under the Australian Pay-As-You-Go (PAYG) taxation system
    • Calculate net earnings after deductions and taxation

Working Mathematically indicators

  • Explain why monthly pay is not four times weekly pay (Converting pay periods)
  • Compare two jobs by converting their pay to the same time period (Converting pay periods, closure)
  • Read the correct row and column of a published table and check that the result is reasonable (PAYG tax table)
  • Carry out a multi-step calculation from yearly salary to net weekly pay and leave pay (Putting it together)

ICT

  • None in this lesson.

LIT

  • Build a "pay words" list on the board as each term appears: per annum, fortnight, annual leave, leave loading, gross, net, tax withheld, tax-free threshold
  • Read the column headings of the ATO tax table aloud with the class and say what each one means in everyday words
  • Students write the answer to the unit question in one full sentence that includes both gross and net weekly pay

Prerequisite knowledge

  • From Lesson 1: wage, salary and commission calculations
  • From Lesson 2: pay = base rate × multiplier × hours
  • Finding a percentage of an amount
  • Reading a table

Resources

  • Worksheet (Q1–Q4 and extension)
  • Exit ticket
  • Calculators
  • Mini whiteboards

Before the lesson

  • Print the tax table extract from the ATO PDF (NAT 1005). Check that the rows used in this lesson are on it: $350, $575, $576, $600, $700, $800, $900, $950, $1,000 and $1,125.

Teaching sequence

Steps & timingTeaching & learning activities
ORIENTATION0–8 minStarter: back to the job adsAfLWM

Teacher

  • Show two retrieval questions, oldest first. Give more time to the one that matches the largest error pile from the Lesson 2 exit tickets.
    1. (Lesson 1) Jess earns a $400 retainer plus 5% commission. This week she sold $6,000 of phones. What is her pay?
    2. (Lesson 2) Ella's base rate is $25. How much does she earn for 4 hours on Saturday at time and a half?
  • (Expected: 1. 0.05 × $6,000 = $300, and $400 + $300 = $700. 2. $25 × 1.5 = $37.50 per hour, and $37.50 × 4 = $150.)
  • Then show today's question, from the Lesson 1 hook: "The barista earns $25 per hour. The office assistant earns $58,500 p.a. Which one earns more?"

Key question: Can we answer today's question yet? What is stopping us?

Expected: Not yet. One is per hour and one is per year. We need to know the barista's hours, and we need both jobs over the same time period.

  • Reveal the learning intention and success criteria. Tell the class that today's question gets answered by the end of the lesson.

Students

  • Answer questions 1 and 2 on mini whiteboards, then suggest what is needed for today's question.
ENHANCING8–20 minConverting pay periodsLITWMAfL

Teacher

  • LIT Add "per annum (p.a.)" and "fortnight" to the pay words list.
  • Model weekly and fortnightly pay for the office assistant. The syllabus uses 1 year = 52 weeks, and 1 fortnight = 2 weeks.

Model: office assistant, $58,500 p.a.

  • Weekly: $58,500 ÷ 52 = $1,125

  • Fortnightly: $1,125 × 2 = $2,250

  • AfL Before showing monthly pay, students estimate the office assistant's monthly pay on whiteboards and hold them up. Write the answers on the board without names. Many will write 4 × $1,125 = $4,500.

  • Then work it out from the yearly amount, using 12 months in a year: $58,500 ÷ 12 = $4,875.

Key question: Why do the two methods give different answers, $4,500 and $4,875?

Expected: A month is not exactly 4 weeks. A year has 52 weeks but only 12 months, so a month is about 52 ÷ 12 ≈ 4.33 weeks. Using 4 weeks leaves out $375 each month.

  • Then model the barista.

Model: barista, $25 per hour, working 38 hours a week

  • Weekly: $25 × 38 = $950
  • Yearly: $950 × 52 = $49,400

Key question: Now can we answer today's question?

Expected: Yes, if the barista works 38 hours a week. The office assistant earns $1,125 per week and the barista earns $950, so the office assistant earns $175 more per week.

  • We do: Jess earns $700 per week. Students find her yearly and monthly pay on whiteboards before the answer is shown. (Expected: yearly $700 × 52 = $36,400, monthly $36,400 ÷ 12 = $3,033.33.)

Students

  • Estimate the monthly pay before it is shown, copy the models, then complete the "we do" on whiteboards.
ENHANCING20–28 minAnnual leave and leave loadingLITWM

Teacher

  • Explain the rules in the Fair Work Ombudsman's words. Full-time and part-time employees get 4 weeks of paid annual leave each year, and casual employees do not (Fair Work Ombudsman, 2026).
  • Some awards also pay an extra amount during leave, called leave loading. In the General Retail Industry Award it is 17.5% (Fair Work Commission, 2026, cl. 28.3).
  • LIT Add "annual leave" and "leave loading" to the pay words list.

Model: Priya is a full-time retail worker covered by the General Retail Industry Award. Her normal pay is $1,000 per week, and she works weekdays only. She takes 4 weeks of annual leave.

  • Normal pay for 4 weeks: $1,000 × 4 = $4,000
  • Leave loading: 17.5% of $4,000 = 0.175 × $4,000 = $700
  • Total paid for her leave: $4,000 + $700 = $4,700

Key question: Is the 17.5% worked out on one week's pay or on the whole leave period?

Expected: On the normal pay for the whole leave period, which here is 4 weeks.

Key question: Ella is a casual barista. Does she get leave loading?

Expected: No. Casual employees do not get paid annual leave, so there is no leave loading for them.

Students

  • Copy the model and answer when named.

Teacher note: Clause 28.3 pays whichever is greater, a 17.5% loading or the penalty rates the worker would have earned for those ordinary hours. Priya works weekdays only, so the 17.5% applies.

ENHANCING28–43 minPAYG tax and the ATO tax tableLITWMAfL

Teacher

  • Explain PAYG in plain words. Under Pay As You Go, the employer takes some tax out of each pay and sends it to the ATO. Gross pay is the pay before tax. Net pay, or take-home pay, is what is left after the tax withheld is taken out.
  • LIT Turn "tax withheld" back into a sentence with the class: "the tax the employer keeps back from each pay".
  • Explain the tax-free threshold with one number. For most Australian residents, the first $18,200 they earn in a year has no income tax (Australian Taxation Office, 2026b). Ask: "Using 52 weeks, how much is that per week?" ($18,200 ÷ 52 = $350.) A worker chooses whether to claim it on the tax file number (TFN) declaration they give their employer (Australian Taxation Office, 2026b).
  • LIT Hand out the tax table extract and read the column headings aloud together: "Weekly earnings", "With tax-free threshold", "No tax-free threshold".
  • Look up $350 together. Column 2 shows $0.00 and column 3 shows $62.00. Ask what the difference tells us.
  • In the rest of this lesson every worker has claimed the tax-free threshold, so we use column 2.

Key question: For a $350 week, why is column 2 $0 but column 3 $62?

Expected: $350 a week is about the tax-free amount, so a worker who has claimed the tax-free threshold has no tax taken out. A worker who has not claimed it has tax taken from every week.

Model: office assistant, gross $1,125 per week

  • Find $1,125 in the "Weekly earnings" column and read column 2. Tax withheld = $178.
  • Net pay = $1,125 − $178 = $947

Key question: Is $178 the tax for the whole year?

Expected: No. It is the tax withheld from one week's pay.

  • We do: Ella's week from Lesson 2 earned $575. Students find the tax withheld and her net pay. (Expected: $36 withheld, net $539.)
  • AfL Circulate. Check that students read across the correct row, and that they subtract the tax instead of adding it. Prompt with: "Should net pay be more or less than gross pay?"

Students

  • Complete the "we do", then Q1–Q4 on their own.

Teacher note: Column 2 stays at $0.00 a little past $350. On this table the first dollar of tax in column 2 is withheld at $365. If students notice, explain that the table is built from ATO formulas, and the exact tax for the year is worked out later in a tax return.

ENHANCING43–50 minPutting it togetherWMAfL

Teacher

  • Set one multi-step problem that uses all three lessons. Students work in pairs, but each student writes their own working.

Students

  • Work in pairs and check each other's table reading.
SYNTHESIZING50–60 minAnswering the unit question, and exit ticketAoLAfLLIT

Teacher

  • Return to the Lesson 1 job ads.

Key question: So which job pays the most: the barista working 38 hours a week, or the office assistant?

Expected: The office assistant. Gross weekly pay is $1,125 against $950. After tax the office assistant takes home $947 and the barista $828 ($950 − $122 withheld).

  • LIT Students write the answer in one full sentence that includes both gross and net pay.
  • Go back to the success criteria. Students tick the ones they can now do on their exit ticket.
  • Hand out the exit ticket (tax table extract and calculator allowed). Collect it before students leave.
  • Preview the next lesson: "The tax withheld each week counts towards the tax you owe for the year. Next we look at how yearly taxable income is worked out."

After the lesson

  • AoL Mark the exit tickets against the success criteria of Lessons 1–3 and record each student's result. Part (d) checks commission (Lesson 1) and penalty rates (Lesson 2).
  • AfL Read the unticked success criteria alongside the answers. Note whether errors are in converting periods, reading the table, or subtracting. Use this to plan the starter for the next lesson.

Adjustments and anticipated misconceptions

Anticipated misconceptions

A month is 4 weeks, so monthly pay = 4 × weekly pay
Why students think thisPeople often say "a month is four weeks", and a 28-day February is exactly 4 weeks.
How this lesson addresses itStudents' own monthly estimates compared with $58,500 ÷ 12. Extension: 52 ÷ 12 ≈ 4.33. Exit ticket (c).
Net pay is more than gross pay, or tax is added
Why students think thisIn everyday English "gross" means disgusting and "net" sounds like a fishing net, so neither word hints at its meaning for pay.
How this lesson addresses itThe gross–net diagram. The prompt "Should net pay be more or less than gross pay?".
Tax withheld from one week is the tax for the year
Why students think thisThe table gives one number and students read it as "the tax".
How this lesson addresses itKey question "Is $178 the tax for the whole year?". Preview of Lesson 3b.
The tax-free threshold means the whole pay is tax-free, or it is a form of tax
Why students think thisThe phrase is abstract and long.
How this lesson addresses itExplained with one number ($18,200 a year = $350 a week), then seen in the table at $350 (column 2 $0, column 3 $62).
Leave loading is worked out on one week's pay
Why students think thisStudents are used to weekly amounts from Lessons 1 and 2.
How this lesson addresses itKey question "one week or the whole leave period?".
Every worker gets annual leave and leave loading
Why students think thisStudents' part-time jobs are often casual, and the rule is not obvious.
How this lesson addresses itKey question about Ella as a casual worker, using the Fair Work Ombudsman wording.

Adjustments

More support
A ruler or strip of paper to read across one row of the tax table. The worksheet starts with Ella's line worked out in full (Net pay = $575 − $36 = $539), then gives "Net pay = ____ − ____" for each question.
More challenge
The extension (weeks in a month) and the full Sam problem.
Language and literacy support, including EAL/D
The pay words list stays on the board with the gross–net diagram. Table headings are read aloud and put into everyday words.
