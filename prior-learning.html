Assumed prior learning, misconceptions and difficulties

Assumed prior learning

NESA lists this background knowledge for Financial mathematics A (NESA, 2022a, Financial mathematics A teaching advice):

  • Converting between percentages, fractions and decimals
  • Calculating fractions, decimals and percentages of quantities
  • Conversion of time periods such as years to months
  • Substitution into formulas
  • Solving equations
  • Reading and interpreting information from tables and graphs

The Lesson 1 diagnostic quiz checks percentages, decimals, time conversions, substitution into a rule and reading a table before they are needed (see Lesson 1). Solving equations is not checked there, because Lessons 1–3 do not use it. Lesson 5 checks it at the start, before students find a missing rate or time.

Technology. We assume students can open a link and complete an online form (Lesson 1 diagnostic quiz), and can enter values and simple formulas in a spreadsheet (Lessons 6, 8 and 9). If they cannot, the teacher gives a short demonstration and a paper version with the same columns.

Earning money (Lessons 1–3)

  • Find a percentage of an amount and change a percentage to a decimal (5% = 0.05)
  • Multiply and divide money amounts, with a calculator, and round to cents
  • Read a simple table, such as a timesheet
  • Students do not need any experience of having a job, a payslip or a tax return

Simple interest (Lessons 4–6)

  • Convert percentages to decimals and find a percentage of an amount
  • Convert time units, substitute into formulas and solve one-step equations
  • Read tables and coordinates, and recognise equal increases (NESA, 2022a)
  • Check these through the Lesson 1 diagnostic evidence, conversions in Lesson 4 and equation readiness in Lesson 5
  • Demonstrate spreadsheet cells if needed, and provide the same task on paper

Spending money (Lessons 7–9)

  • Calculate a percentage of an amount and convert common percentages to decimals, for example 20% = 0.20.

  • Add, subtract, multiply and divide money amounts; use a calculator and round monetary results to cents.

  • Convert stated durations into payment counts where the frequency is given, such as one year of monthly payments = 12 payments.

  • Read a simple table or timeline and distinguish a one-off amount from a recurring payment.

  • Recognise income and expenses from the preceding Earning money lessons. Do not assume that students have personal experience of credit products.

  • For spreadsheet activities, enter values, identify cell references and use basic addition/multiplication formulas. Confirm this familiarity beforehand; provide brief instruction or the paper equivalent if needed.

  • Check these assumptions through Lesson 7 retrieval and the early checks in Lessons 8–9. An isolated arithmetic slip should receive different feedback from a repeated structural error.

Anticipated misconceptions and difficulties

These are planning predictions, to be checked against what students actually write.

Earning money (Lessons 1–3)

Misconception Why students think this How it is identified Teaching response
5% is written as 0.5 They move the decimal point once instead of dividing by 100. Lesson 1 diagnostic Q1 Key question "Why 0.05 and not 0.5?" in the commission example
Commission is worked out on the retainer "Commission" is a new word, and the retainer is the first number they see. Lesson 1 You do Q5, exit ticket (b) Key question "Is the 5% worked out on the $400 or the $6,000?"
Time and a half means adding $1.50 "And a half" sounds like adding something small. Lesson 2 worked example, exit ticket (b) Reference box links each phrase to a multiplier. Key question "$37.50 or $26.50?"
Hours are added instead of multiplied Two numbers on a line get combined the quickest way. Lesson 2 Spot the Mistake Students explain Jayden's error in a "because" sentence
All overtime hours are paid at the same rate Most examples use one rate for the whole shift. Lesson 2 overtime example Real pay guide headings: first 2 hours, then after 2 hours
Monthly pay = 4 × weekly pay Calendars are often drawn as 4 rows of weeks. Lesson 3 key question, exit ticket (c) 52 ÷ 12 ≈ 4.33 weeks in a month
Net pay is more than gross pay, or tax is added "Gross" and "net" give no clue to their meaning in everyday English. Lesson 3 You do Gross–net diagram. Prompt "Should net pay be more or less than gross pay?"
The tax-free threshold means the whole pay is tax-free The phrase is long and abstract. Lesson 3 key question at $350 $18,200 ÷ 52 = $350, then the table at $350: $0 with the threshold, $62 without
Every worker gets annual leave and leave loading Students' own jobs are usually casual. Lesson 3 key question about Ella Fair Work Ombudsman wording: casual employees do not get paid annual leave

Simple interest (Lessons 4–6)

These are planning predictions. Check reading, meaning and working before reteaching (NSW Department of Education, n.d.).

Misconception Why students think this How it is identified Teaching response
5% = 0.5 Students may remember a percentage as a whole number or move the decimal one place. Lesson 4 starter Reconnect 5/100 to 0.05
18 months = 1.8 years Decimal notation can be confused with a year-and-month label. Lesson 4 practice Divide the months by 12
Interest = total amount Both are amounts of money, and “how much” is ambiguous without reading the noun. Lesson 4 exit ticket Label A = P + I
Annual interest increases each year Everyday “growth” language can suggest that all money in the account earns interest. Lesson 4 table Return to the original principal
r = 0.015 means 0.015% Decimal rate and percentage notation may be confused. Lesson 5 exit ticket Multiply the decimal by 100
An unknown is found with the wrong operation Students may recall the direct formula without interpreting the substituted equation. Lesson 5 working Substitute, divide, then verify
The amount graph starts at zero Students may recall I = 0 at time zero and transfer this to A. Lesson 5 graph Identify A when n = 0
The gradient is the total rise or a percentage Students may subtract two amounts but omit division by the interval. A coefficient and percentage rate are both described as rates, but have different units. Lesson 6 matching Calculate dollars per year

Spending money (Lessons 7–9)

These are planning hypotheses, not findings about an observed class. Confirm them through student responses.

Potential misconception How it will be identified Teaching response
Uses 20 rather than 0.20 in a percentage calculation. Lesson 7 retrieval: 20% of $900. Use 10% as one tenth, build 20%, then reconnect to multiplication by 0.20.
Omits the deposit or treats it as a monthly payment. Lesson 7 hinge and labelled working. Separate deposit, repayment total and total paid; show the one-off deposit on a timeline.
Counts years instead of monthly payments. Checks on payment count before multiplication. Write duration × payments per unit of time first; one year monthly means 12 payments.
Assumes a smaller monthly repayment is cheaper. Lesson 7 comparison: $1,500 versus $1,680. Compare complete totals and explain the different durations; distinguish total cost from payment size.
Adds the payment today twice. Lesson 8 retrieval: four payments including today. Mark today as payment 1 of 4, rather than an extra payment.
Assumes interest-free means no additional charges. Lesson 8 hinge, advertisement discussion and exit. Read every stated condition; compute principal payments and fees separately.
Confuses a per-payment charge with a total charge. Hand calculation and spreadsheet audit in Lesson 8. Label the charge basis; multiply only recurring charges by their stated count.
Treats a late fee as replacing the missed payment. Compare on-time and one-late-payment scenarios. Retain all required payments and add only the explicitly stated extra fee.
Applies a loan fee percentage to the wrong amount. Lesson 9 model and hinge. Circle “original principal”; state the percentage base before calculation.
Calls total repaid the borrowing cost. Lesson 9 retrieval and independent assessment. Use borrowed / returned / extra rows; borrowing cost = total repaid − principal.
Adds fees twice when already included in repayments. Check consistency between total and payment schedule. Calculate principal plus fees once, then distribute that total into repayments.
Equates meeting payment limits with affordability. Buyer-profile explanations in all three lessons. Require separate claims about cost, schedule fit and missing income/expense information.
Trusts spreadsheet output without checking the formula. Compare the formula with hand calculations. Audit cell references and charge basis; test a known case before varying an input.
