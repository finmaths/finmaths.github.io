Assumed prior learning

The NSW Education Standards Authority (NESA) lists this background knowledge for Financial mathematics A (NESA, 2022a, Financial mathematics A, Teaching advice section). The right-hand column shows the earlier outcome that covers each skill.

Calculating fractions, decimals and percentages of quantities
Earlier outcome that covers itMA4-FRC-C-01 (as above)
Conversion of time periods such as years to months
Earlier outcome that covers itNo single outcome (see the note below)
Substitution into formulas
Earlier outcome that covers itMA4-ALG-C-01: generalises number properties to operate with algebraic expressions including expansion and factorisation
Solving equations
Earlier outcome that covers itMA4-EQU-C-01: solves linear equations of up to 2 steps and quadratic equations of the form ax² = c
Reading and interpreting information from tables and graphs

The outcome codes and wording are from the same syllabus (NESA, 2022a). We added a code only where the outcome clearly covers the skill. The Stage 3 time outcome is about 12- and 24-hour time, not converting years to months, so that row has no code.

The Lesson 1 diagnostic quiz checks percentages, decimals, time conversions, substitution into a rule and reading a table before they are needed (see Lesson 1). Solving equations is not checked there, because Lessons 1–3 do not use it. Lesson 5 checks it at the start, before students find a missing rate or time.

Technology. We assume students can open a link and complete an online form (Lesson 1 diagnostic quiz), and can enter values and simple formulas in a spreadsheet (Lessons 6, 8 and 9). If they cannot, the teacher gives a short demonstration and a paper version with the same columns.

Earning money (Lessons 1–3)

  • Find a percentage of an amount and change a percentage to a decimal (5% = 0.05)
  • Multiply and divide money amounts, with a calculator, and round to cents
  • Read a simple table, such as a timesheet
  • Students do not need any experience of having a job, a payslip or a tax return

Simple interest (Lessons 4–6)

  • Convert percentages to decimals and find a percentage of an amount
  • Convert time units, substitute into formulas and solve one-step equations
  • Read tables and coordinates, and recognise equal increases
  • Check these through the Lesson 1 diagnostic evidence, conversions in Lesson 4 and equation readiness in Lesson 5
  • Demonstrate spreadsheet cells if needed, and provide the same task on paper

Spending money (Lessons 7–9)

  • Calculate a percentage of an amount and convert common percentages to decimals, for example 20% = 0.20.

  • Add, subtract, multiply and divide money amounts; use a calculator and round monetary results to cents.

  • Convert stated durations into payment counts where the frequency is given, such as one year of monthly payments = 12 payments.

  • Read a simple table or timeline and distinguish a one-off amount from a recurring payment.

  • Recognise income and expenses from the preceding Earning money lessons. Do not assume that students have personal experience of credit products.

  • For spreadsheet activities, enter values, identify cell references and use basic addition/multiplication formulas. Confirm this familiarity beforehand; provide brief instruction or the paper equivalent if needed.

  • Check these assumptions through Lesson 7 retrieval and the early checks in Lessons 8–9. An isolated arithmetic slip should receive different feedback from a repeated structural error.

Anticipated misconceptions and difficulties

These are planning predictions, to be checked against what students actually write.

Earning money (Lessons 1–3)

5% is written as 0.5
Why students think thisThey move the decimal point once instead of dividing by 100.
How it is identifiedLesson 1 diagnostic Q1
Teaching responseKey question "Why 0.05 and not 0.5?" in the commission example
Commission is worked out on the retainer
Why students think this"Commission" is a new word, and the retainer is the first number they see.
How it is identifiedLesson 1 You do Q5, exit ticket (b)
Teaching responseKey question "Is the 5% worked out on the $400 or the $6,000?"
Time and a half means adding $1.50
Why students think this"And a half" sounds like adding something small.
How it is identifiedLesson 2 worked example, exit ticket (b)
Teaching responseReference box links each phrase to a multiplier. Key question "$37.50 or $26.50?"
Hours are added instead of multiplied
Why students think thisTwo numbers on a line get combined the quickest way.
How it is identifiedLesson 2 Spot the Mistake
Teaching responseStudents explain Jayden's error in a "because" sentence
All overtime hours are paid at the same rate
Why students think thisMost examples use one rate for the whole shift.
How it is identifiedLesson 2 overtime example
Teaching responseReal pay guide headings: first 2 hours, then after 2 hours
Monthly pay = 4 × weekly pay
Why students think thisCalendars are often drawn as 4 rows of weeks.
How it is identifiedLesson 3 key question, exit ticket (c)
Teaching response52 ÷ 12 ≈ 4.33 weeks in a month
Net pay is more than gross pay, or tax is added
Why students think this"Gross" and "net" give no clue to their meaning in everyday English.
How it is identifiedLesson 3 You do
Teaching responseGross–net diagram. Prompt "Should net pay be more or less than gross pay?"
The tax-free threshold means the whole pay is tax-free
Why students think thisThe phrase is long and abstract.
How it is identifiedLesson 3 key question at $350
Teaching response$18,200 ÷ 52 = $350, then the table at $350: $0 with the threshold, $62 without
Every worker gets annual leave and leave loading
Why students think thisStudents' own jobs are usually casual.
How it is identifiedLesson 3 key question about Ella
Teaching responseFair Work Ombudsman wording: casual employees do not get paid annual leave

Simple interest (Lessons 4–6)

Treat these as planning predictions. Newman's error analysis distinguishes reading, comprehension, transformation, processing and encoding; use brief questions to locate the difficulty before choosing a response (NSW Department of Education, n.d., introductory section).

5% = 0.5
Why students think thisStudents may remember a percentage as a whole number or move the decimal one place.
How it is identifiedLesson 4 starter
Teaching responseReconnect 5/100 to 0.05
18 months = 1.8 years
Why students think thisDecimal notation can be confused with a year-and-month label.
How it is identifiedLesson 4 practice
Teaching responseUse 18 ÷ 12 = 1.5 years
Interest = total amount
Why students think thisBoth are amounts of money, and “how much” is ambiguous without reading the noun.
How it is identifiedLesson 4 exit ticket
Teaching responseLabel A = P + I
Annual interest increases each year
Why students think thisEveryday “growth” language can suggest that all money in the account earns interest.
How it is identifiedLesson 4 table
Teaching responseReturn to the original principal and fixed rate
r = 0.015 means 0.015%
Why students think thisDecimal rate and percentage notation may be confused.
How it is identifiedLesson 5 exit ticket
Teaching responseMultiply the decimal by 100 to obtain 1.5%
An unknown is found with the wrong operation
Why students think thisStudents may recall the direct formula without interpreting the substituted equation.
How it is identifiedLesson 5 working
Teaching responseSubstitute known values, solve the resulting equation and verify
The amount graph starts at zero
Why students think thisStudents may recall I = 0 at time zero and transfer this to A.
How it is identifiedLesson 5 graph
Teaching responseIdentify A = P when n = 0
The gradient is the total rise or a percentage
Why students think thisStudents may subtract two amounts but omit division by the interval. A coefficient and percentage rate are both described as rates, but have different units.
How it is identifiedLesson 6 matching
Teaching responseCalculate change in amount ÷ change in time, in dollars per year

Spending money (Lessons 7–9)

These are anticipated explanations, not observed diagnoses. Use the listed checks to distinguish mathematical misunderstandings from language interpretation or arithmetic slips; adjust teaching based on the evidence.

Uses 20 rather than 0.20 in a percentage calculation.
Why students may think thisPercent notation is treated as a whole number rather than a rate out of 100; the decimal conversion has not become secure.
How to identify itLesson 7 retrieval: 20% of $900.
Teaching responseUse 10% as one tenth, build 20%, then reconnect to multiplication by 0.20.
Omits the deposit or treats it as a monthly payment.
Why students may think thisStudents apply a familiar repeated-payment calculation but fail to coordinate the distinct initial payment; deposit language may be unfamiliar.
How to identify itLesson 7 hinge and labelled working.
Teaching responseSeparate deposit, repayment total and total paid; show the one-off deposit on a timeline.
Counts years instead of monthly payments.
Why students may think thisThey use the duration number directly, without translating its unit into the payment frequency.
How to identify itChecks on payment count before multiplication.
Teaching responseWrite duration × payments per unit of time first; one year monthly means 12 payments.
Assumes a smaller monthly repayment is cheaper.
Why students may think thisThe smaller, prominent monthly figure is more immediately visible than the cumulative effect of a longer term.
How to identify itLesson 7 comparison: $1,500 versus $1,680.
Teaching responseCompare complete totals and explain the different durations; distinguish total cost from payment size.
Adds the payment today twice.
Why students may think thisThey interpret today as separate from the advertised count, missing the inclusive meaning of the wording.
How to identify itLesson 8 retrieval: four payments including today.
Teaching responseMark today as payment 1 of 4, rather than an extra payment.
Assumes interest-free means no additional charges.
Why students may think thisThey generalise absence of interest to absence of every charge, or attend only to the headline.
How to identify itLesson 8 hinge, advertisement discussion and exit.
Teaching responseRead every stated condition; compute principal payments and fees separately.
Confuses a per-payment charge with a total charge.
Why students may think thisThey overlook whether a stated charge applies once or at every scheduled date.
How to identify itHand calculation and spreadsheet audit in Lesson 8.
Teaching responseLabel the charge basis; multiply only recurring charges by their stated count.
Treats a late fee as replacing the missed payment.
Why students may think thisThey treat the penalty as an alternative payment rather than a charge added to the original obligation.
How to identify itCompare on-time and one-late-payment scenarios.
Teaching responseRetain all required payments and add only the explicitly stated extra fee.
Applies a loan fee percentage to the wrong amount.
Why students may think thisThey use the nearest number or transfer a reducing-balance assumption, without checking the contractual base specified in the task.
How to identify itLesson 9 model and hinge.
Teaching responseCircle “original principal”; state the percentage base before calculation.
Calls total repaid the borrowing cost.
Why students may think thisThey treat all money returned as expense and fail to distinguish returning the principal from paying extra.
How to identify itLesson 9 retrieval and independent assessment.
Teaching responseUse borrowed / returned / extra rows; borrowing cost = total repaid − principal.
Adds fees twice when already included in repayments.
Why students may think thisThey combine two representations of the same fees: the total-cost calculation and instalments that already include those fees.
How to identify itCheck consistency between total and payment schedule.
Teaching responseCalculate principal plus fees once, then distribute that total into repayments.
Equates meeting payment limits with affordability.
Why students may think thisThey focus on the two supplied ceilings and assume the omitted financial information has no effect.
How to identify itBuyer-profile explanations in all three lessons.
Teaching responseRequire separate claims about cost, schedule fit and missing income/expense information.
Trusts spreadsheet output without checking the formula.
Why students may think thisA calculator-like view of the spreadsheet encourages trust in the displayed number without checking cell references or the model.
How to identify itCompare the formula with hand calculations.
Teaching responseAudit cell references and charge basis; test a known case before varying an input.
