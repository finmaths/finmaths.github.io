What the unit covers

The unit teaches the three parts of the Stage 5 Financial mathematics A content, which are earning money, simple interest and spending money (NSW Education Standards Authority [NESA], 2022a).

Students solve problems with wages, penalty rates, commission, pay periods, leave loading, PAYG tax, simple interest, buying on terms, buy now pay later, and short-term loans.

Why it is important

Students are already earning. Financial mathematics is percentage and rate work in a setting students are about to meet. The PISA 2022 financial literacy framework notes that "many 15-year-old students are also already engaged in some form of paid work outside school hours" (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2023, Chapter 3, Education and work section). It lists "understanding payslips" as a typical task in this context (OECD, 2023, Chapter 3, Education and work section).

The content matches what the OECD expects. The same framework names the kinds of income taught in Lessons 1–3, including "salary, commission" (OECD, 2023, Chapter 3, Planning and managing finances section). It also expects students to "understand different ways of discussing income (such as hourly wage and gross or net annual income)" (OECD, 2023, Chapter 3, Planning and managing finances section).

It needs mathematics. The framework notes that "some financial decisions may require people to perform some basic calculation, such as percentages" (OECD, 2023, Chapter 3, The interaction of financial literacy with knowledge and skills in other domains section). This is why the content belongs in mathematics.

It leads to later study. Taxable income is "revisited and explored further in the taxation section of the Stage 6 Mathematics Standard syllabus" (NESA, 2022a, Financial mathematics A teaching advice). NESA also links the content to using spreadsheets for wages, overtime, PAYG tax and net earnings.

Pedagogical approach

Earning money (Lessons 1–3)

One question runs through all three lessons

In the lessons. Lesson 1 asks "Which job pays the most?" and shows that it cannot be answered yet. Lesson 3 answers it with gross and net weekly pay. This gives students a reason to learn pay periods and tax, not just a list of skills.

Why. "Pays the most" is not yet clear. NESA's language guidance points out that in a comparison "the term 'better' can be interpreted in multiple ways" (NESA, 2022b, Hypothetical comparisons section). Students have to decide whether they mean per hour, per week or after tax before they can answer.

The same people come back

In the lessons. Ella the barista, Jess the phone salesperson and the office assistant from Lesson 1 reappear in Lessons 2 and 3, so each lesson starts from numbers students already know.

Why. This follows NESA's advice of "providing students with a problem they are familiar with before introducing more complex problems" (NESA, 2022b, Unpack and scaffold the use of language section).

Students check their work against real documents

In the lessons. In Lesson 2 students check $26.44 × 1.5 against the overtime rate printed in a Fair Work Ombudsman pay guide. In Lesson 3 they read the ATO weekly tax table that employers use from 1 July 2026.

Why. This is the "investigate sources of published tables" that the syllabus asks for (NESA, 2022a).

Each new idea is shown once in a fully worked example before students try it

In the lessons. Every step is written on the board as the class watches, and key questions ask why each step is done. Guided practice ("We do") then comes before independent practice ("You do").

Why. CESE recommends that teachers "complete worked examples in 'real time', rather than showing students pre-prepared solutions or responses" (Centre for Education Statistics and Evaluation [CESE], 2020, p. 11). It also places guided practice before independent practice.

Assessment information moves forward

In the lessons. The Lesson 1 diagnostic quiz is mapped to the skills Lessons 2 and 3 need. Each exit ticket is sorted by type of error, and the largest group decides the next starter.

Why. CESE advises teachers at the start of a unit to "use formative assessment to assess previously covered skills, concepts or content knowledge, so that lessons can be adjusted or paced to students' skills and knowledge" (CESE, 2020, p. 11). Wiliam (2011) argues that "for assessment to support learning, it must provide guidance about the next steps in instruction" (p. 13). The sorted exit tickets are how these lessons do that.

Abstract words are treated as misconceptions

In the lessons. Terms such as commission, penalty rate, gross, net and tax-free threshold are new to most students. Each lesson's vocabulary list pairs the key terms with a photo or a diagram, and Lesson 3 builds a "pay words" list on the board as each term appears.

Why. NESA advises that "pre-teaching vocabulary reduces the cognitive load of reading a task" (NESA, 2022b, Explicitly teaching terminology section). It also advises that "as each word or phrase is introduced", teachers "show an image … and explain what it means in context" (NESA, 2022b, Explicitly teaching terminology section).

Working Mathematically is built into the content, not added on

In the lessons. Each Working Mathematically indicator is attached to a named activity, for example explaining Jayden's error in a "because" sentence in Lesson 2.

Why. NESA states that "the Working mathematically processes should be embedded within the concepts being taught" (NESA, 2023, Overarching Working mathematically outcome section).

Simple interest (Lessons 4–6)

Saving builds on percentage work

In the lessons. Lesson 4 opens with Ella's $1000 savings in a fictional account paying 5% p.a. Before any formula appears, students write 5% as a decimal and find 5% of $1000.

Why. The OECD framework quoted above notes that financial decisions can require basic calculations such as percentages (OECD, 2023). Simple interest applies the percentage skills from Lessons 1–3 to saving.

Ella and the same rule come back

In the lessons. Ella, the barista from Lessons 1–3, is the saver in Lesson 4. The rule A = 2000 + 120n is graphed in Lesson 5 and interpreted again in Lesson 6.

Why. Students meet each new step with numbers they already know, the same reason given for Lessons 1–3 above.

Worked examples come before independent work

In the lessons. In Lesson 4 the teacher models M1 and M2 on the board, the class works W1–W4 together, and students then answer Y1–Y4 on their own.

Why. This sequence of teacher model, guided practice and independent work follows Rosenshine (2012).

Graphs are drawn in one lesson and interpreted in the next

In the lessons. Lesson 5 builds the first amount graph by hand, from formula to table to points. Lesson 6 starts from that same graph to explain the initial value and the gradient.

Why. Spreading the graph work over two lessons limits how much is new at one time (CESE, 2018).

Rule, table and graph are linked

In the lessons. In Lesson 6 pairs predict how changing the rate to 4%, 6% or 8% changes the table and graph, check in the spreadsheet, and verify one row by hand. They then match rules, tables and graphs.

Why. Students link the three representations instead of treating them as separate tasks, and predict before the spreadsheet shows the answer (Ainsworth, 2006).

Key words are taught with diagrams

In the lessons. Principal, interest and total amount are shown on a labelled money bar in Lesson 4, and gradient on an annotated graph in Lesson 6.

Why. This follows the same NESA vocabulary advice quoted for Lessons 1–3 (NESA, 2022b).

Working Mathematically is part of each task

In the lessons. Students generalise Ella's table to I = Prn (Lesson 4), check a missing rate by substitution (Lessons 5 and 6), and explain why simple-interest growth is linear (Lesson 6).

Why. This follows the same NESA advice quoted for Lessons 1–3 (NESA, 2023).

Errors decide the next step

In the lessons. Exit tickets and independent work are sorted by type of error: rate, time, amount or interest, and base. The result chooses the opening retrieval for the next lesson. Lesson 6 ends with an independent 8-mark checkpoint: rule (1), table and graph (2), interpretation (2), missing rate and check (2), and linearity (1).

Why. The next correction is chosen from the errors students actually make (Black & Wiliam, 1998).

Spending money (Lessons 7–9)

Spending money connects percentage and money calculations to purchase decisions

Students learn to examine complete costs rather than relying on a prominent monthly payment or an “interest-free” headline. The three lessons address the purchase-plan, BNPL and small-loan content within Financial mathematics A (NSW Education Standards Authority [NESA], 2022a). The OECD (2023) financial-literacy framework places financial knowledge and skills in decision-making contexts; this supports teaching students to interpret offers as well as calculate their costs.

The sequence increases complexity gradually

Lesson 7 begins with fictional fee-free plans so students can coordinate a deposit, repeated payments and duration. Lesson 8 adds explicitly stated charges and conditions. Lesson 9 applies the total-cost reasoning to borrowing. This separates the initial calculation structure from later interpretation of more detailed terms. This is a planned application of cognitive load theory: labelled calculation rows and gradual introduction of terms aim to reduce avoidable processing demands while students learn the core structure (Sweller et al., 2019).

Worked examples make the calculation structure visible

In Lesson 7, the $900 teacher model uses different values from the $1,200 comparison task, so students must transfer the method. Explanation prompts, such as why the deposit is counted once, encourage understanding rather than copying. This applies Renkl’s (2014) account of example-based learning and active explanation.

Contrasting offers require mathematical reasoning

A smaller repayment can produce a larger total when payments continue longer. Students must support a comparison with numbers and distinguish lower total cost from fitting a stated payment ceiling. A conclusion about genuine affordability also requires information about other commitments; it cannot follow from a payment ceiling alone.

Assessment informs teaching decisions

Retrieval questions, simultaneous hinge responses and individual exit tickets reveal errors before the next task. The teacher uses the pattern of responses to reteach a specific difficulty and then checks a fresh example. This is the intended application of Black and Wiliam’s (2009) formative-assessment framework, rather than collecting answers without changing instruction.

Literacy support is part of the mathematical work

Students annotate terms such as “including today”, “per month” and “original amount borrowed”. Vocabulary definitions, labelled calculation rows and sentence frames help students interpret the problem and communicate a numerical justification. Individual writing before partner talk preserves evidence of each student’s thinking.

Technology helps students investigate relationships

In Lessons 8 and 9, students first calculate by hand, then construct and audit spreadsheet formulas. They change a fee or duration, predict the effect and explain the resulting totals. The spreadsheet is therefore used to test a mathematical model; paper tables provide an alternative with the same reasoning demand.

Fictional buyer profiles allow discussion without disclosure of family finances

Scaffolds address percentage fluency, payment counting and language barriers while retaining the common learning goals. Extensions investigate equal-cost conditions. All offers are labelled fictional, so the tasks do not imply current product terms or legal fee limits.
